**Read this guide before completing your Week 3 assignment.** You do not need to read three full CDC pages or a research paper to begin. Read Sections 1–5 here, then use the linked original sections when you need to verify a fact. The material below is an explanation based on the sources, not a reproduction of their articles.

## 1. Understanding the disease stages

You have already worked with SIR and SEIR models. In Week 3, you will use the same town to compare diseases, so you need to understand what the stages represent before changing any numbers. In an SEIR simulation, **S** stands for susceptible, meaning that you have not been infected and can still become infected. **E** stands for exposed, but its meaning in mathematical modeling is more specific than in everyday conversation: the person is already infected but cannot yet transmit the infection. **I** stands for infectious, meaning that the person can transmit the infection to someone else. **R** stands for recovered or removed from the chain of transmission. In our simple town model, you assume a recovered resident cannot become infected again. That is a model rule, not a universal statement about immunity to every disease.

Donald S. Burke's 2024 paper explains an important vocabulary problem. Public health agencies often use *exposed* to describe someone who came into contact with an infectious person and **might** be infected. Most SEIR models instead use E for someone who **is** infected but is not yet infectious. Burke argues that the word *latent* would be more accurate for the modeling stage. This distinction matters because you must not change a resident from S to E merely because a meeting happened. In our simulation, the infection must pass during that meeting before you mark the resident E. A resident then stays E for the chosen latent period and becomes I after that period ends.


There are three periods you should keep separate. **Incubation period** means the time from infection until symptoms begin. **Latent or exposed period** in our SEIR model means the time from infection until the person becomes infectious. **Infectious period** means the time during which an infected person can spread the pathogen. These periods are not always the same. A person may start spreading a disease before symptoms appear, so the incubation period can be longer than the latent period. This is one reason you cannot automatically enter a CDC incubation number into the `exposed_days` variable.

## 2. Measles

**Primary source:** [CDC, Clinical Overview of Measles](https://www.cdc.gov/measles/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html), updated August 5, 2026. Use the *Clinical features*, *How it spreads*, and *Prevention* sections.

Measles is a highly contagious viral respiratory illness. The CDC explains that people can spread the virus through the air when they breathe, cough, or sneeze, and that direct contact with infectious droplets can also transmit it. The agency states that measles virus can remain infectious in the air for up to two hours after an infected person leaves a space. That means transmission does not necessarily require two people to be face to face at exactly the same time. Our Week 2 town is much simpler: an infectious resident makes random meetings, and a susceptible resident has a chance of becoming infected during a meeting. You can still use this town to explore hypothetical differences in transmission, but you should explicitly explain that the meeting rule does not recreate all airborne transmission pathways.

The CDC describes a typical illness that begins with fever, malaise, cough, runny nose, and inflamed eyes, followed by a rash. It says the rash usually appears around 14 days after exposure. More importantly for disease spread, a person can be contagious before anyone sees that characteristic rash. The CDC describes a contagious window extending from four days before the rash appears through four days afterward. Because infectiousness can begin before the rash, the timing of symptoms cannot be used as a shortcut for deciding when residents become infectious. **Brief wording from the CDC's *Clinical features* section:** “The rash usually appears about 14 days after a person is exposed.”

The CDC also reports that as many as nine in ten susceptible people who have **close contact** with a measles patient may develop measles. This is a statement about a particular type of exposure, not a universal probability for one randomly selected meeting in our simulation. You should not type `0.90` into `infection_chance` and claim that the CDC validated it for our model. Our town does not specify how close a meeting is, how long it lasts, whether the people share indoor air, or how the vaccination status changes susceptibility. Each of those details affects how well a close-contact statistic could apply. A research-based comparison must state the limits of this translation.

Vaccination is another important distinction. The CDC identifies the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine as the best protection against measles and its complications. For our project, vaccination may later appear as a slider that changes how many residents are susceptible. But you need to decide and document the rule: does a vaccinated resident start outside the susceptible group, or does vaccination reduce that resident's probability of infection? These are different model choices. Do not claim a precise vaccine effectiveness number unless you obtain a source that supports it in the population and context you are modeling.

**What to record for Week 3:** the route of transmission, the reported timing of rash and contagiousness, the exact CDC section, and which model inputs remain unsupported by this page. In particular, keep `infection_chance`, `exposed_days`, and `infectious_days` separate rather than treating them as automatic copies of a single CDC statement.

## 3. Influenza (flu)

**Primary source:** [CDC Yellow Book, Influenza](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/travel-associated-infections-diseases/influenza.html), dated April 23, 2025. Read the *Infectious agent*, *Transmission*, and *Prevention* sections. This source addresses influenza generally, including different circulating viruses, so do not assume every influenza strain has identical characteristics.

Influenza is a respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses. The CDC Yellow Book explains that influenza A and influenza B commonly cause seasonal epidemics in people, while other influenza virus types have different patterns of infection. The composition of circulating influenza viruses can differ from year to year and between places. For this project, it is acceptable to model a simplified seasonal influenza scenario, but you should describe it as *seasonal influenza* rather than treating every influenza subtype or strain as identical. This is especially important if you later compare a disease-specific number taken from one study with numbers from very different populations or years.

Influenza usually spreads through respiratory particles released when an infectious person coughs or sneezes near someone who is susceptible. The CDC also discusses smaller airborne particles in confined spaces and indirect transmission when contaminated hands contact the face. Our town simplifies all of these mechanisms to encounters between residents. A more realistic model might depend on how close people stand, where they meet, and how long they remain together. Since we are not building that kind of detailed model, we should describe the meeting probability as a **model assumption** unless we can justify it using research specific to the kind of contact we simulate.

The CDC Yellow Book reports that influenza symptoms typically appear one to four days after exposure. Many adults can be infectious beginning the day before symptoms start and remain infectious for approximately five to seven days after symptoms begin. The source adds that infectiousness is greatest during the first several days of illness, and that children, immunocompromised people, and people with severe illness may shed virus longer. A person can also transmit influenza without having symptoms. This means there is no single exposed or infectious duration that fits every infected person. **Brief wording from the CDC's *Transmission* section:** “The incubation period is usually 1–4 days after exposure.”

For our town, you could represent a simplified influenza scenario with fixed numbers of exposed and infectious days, as Week 2 already does for a teaching outbreak. But you must label those chosen fixed numbers as modeling assumptions unless the source directly supports how they were selected. Do not treat the one-to-four-day *incubation* range as a verified one-to-four-day *latent* period. If you use an infectious duration drawn from the CDC's adult timeline, state that its timing is measured relative to symptom onset, not simply from the time a resident becomes infectious in our code. The definitions must match before you use the values.

The CDC also explains that influenza vaccination is an important way to prevent influenza and its complications. In a later slider assignment, we may examine whether higher vaccination levels reduce the number of infections in the simulation. The graph will show what happens **under our chosen assumptions**, not a validated real-world estimate of a particular seasonal vaccine's performance. The same caution applies to any stay-home or isolation setting in the demo.

**What to record for Week 3:** the disease type being described, transmission route, incubation range, infectious timing relative to symptoms, relevant source section, and limits on using these facts as direct model parameters.

## 4. COVID-19

**Primary source:** [CDC, Clinical Presentation: Clinical considerations for care of children and adults with confirmed COVID-19](https://www.cdc.gov/covid/hcp/clinical-care/covid19-presentation.html), dated February 5, 2026. Read *Key points*, *Incubation period*, *Presentation*, and *Transmission*.

COVID-19 is caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus. The CDC explains that the clinical course can vary widely: someone may have no symptoms, develop mild or moderate illness, or become critically ill. The page also explains that people who are vaccinated or have had prior infections may still become infected. For our educational town, however, we use only four health states and do not simulate hospitalization, symptom severity, testing, or multiple reinfections. You should therefore understand the difference between what the CDC describes clinically and what our simplified code represents. A good simulation clearly states which aspects of reality it includes and which it leaves out.

One especially important finding for modeling is that people can transmit SARS-CoV-2 **before symptoms begin**. The CDC also notes that transmission can occur from people without symptoms, people who have not yet developed symptoms, and people who are symptomatic. In its transmission discussion, the CDC describes high transmissibility around the period just before symptoms and the first days afterward, and notes that viral shedding can last up to about ten days for many people. **Brief wording from the CDC's *Key points* section:** “Infected people can transmit SARS-CoV-2 ... before symptom onset.” This is why you must not treat the onset of symptoms as the same event as becoming infectious.

The source makes another point that matters for your assignment: **COVID-19 incubation periods depend on the variant and the study period.** The CDC reports that meta-analyses of studies published in 2020 found a pooled mean incubation period of approximately 6.5 days. It also reports a study from a period with substantial Delta transmission estimating around 4.3 days, and a median of about three to four days in studies during substantial Omicron transmission. These numbers are not interchangeable. The 6.5-day figure is a mean from a group of earlier studies, while the Omicron figure is reported as a median from a different period. You should not average these figures and claim the resulting number is a general COVID-19 parameter. Instead, choose and describe a specific scenario if the project eventually requires one set of numbers.

The difference between incubation and latency matters here as well. An incubation estimate tells you about the time until symptoms, not necessarily the moment infectiousness begins. A COVID-19 model that uses `exposed_days` to represent the time until infectiousness therefore cannot automatically substitute the incubation values above. Similarly, the source's discussion of viral shedding does not establish that every resident remains equally infectious for exactly ten days. Our current town assumes fixed periods and the same infectiousness each day, and these are deliberate simplifications that should be stated in our findings.

In Week 4, our project plan proposes sliders for vaccination and staying home while sick. Those sliders can help people explore what happens when the model changes the number of susceptible contacts or the frequency of meetings. But their effects will depend on the rules we program. If moving a slider reduces infections, that demonstrates how **our model** responds, not a direct CDC estimate of the real-world effectiveness of that intervention for all COVID-19 variants.

**What to record for Week 3:** the fact that transmission can begin before symptoms, the variant-specific incubation estimates with their different statistical descriptions, the source's discussion of infectious timing, and the values that the clinical page does **not** directly supply for our simulation.

## 5. How to move from research to simulation inputs

Our Week 2 town uses a population of 100 residents, three random meetings per infectious person per day, a 0.15 probability of transmission per eligible meeting, five exposed days, ten infectious days, and a fixed random seed. These are values selected for a teaching simulation. They are not automatically estimates of measles, influenza, or COVID-19. Week 3 research is intended to prepare us to compare disease scenarios, not to relabel those Week 2 numbers as medically measured values.

Use three labels whenever you propose a value. **SOURCED** means you copied a quantity accurately from a named source and recorded its meaning, units, and context. **DERIVED** means you calculated a new quantity from documented inputs and showed the calculation and assumptions. **ASSUMPTION** means you chose a value for the simulation and have not established it as a measured property of the disease. A sourced number can still be unsuitable for a particular model input if it measures something different.

For example, the basic reproduction number, commonly written **R0**, describes expected secondary infections under specified conditions in a population where people are susceptible. It is not the probability that infection passes in one arbitrary encounter. Converting R0 to a meeting-level probability requires additional assumptions about contact patterns, infectious duration, and the model's transmission process. Similarly, an incubation period does not automatically equal the model's latent period, and a window of infectiousness measured relative to symptoms cannot be inserted unchanged as a fixed number of infectious days. If you cannot justify a conversion, leave the input as **not yet determined** and tell the project leader what is missing.

| Our town input | What you need to establish | Research limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `infection_chance` | Probability of transmission per eligible simulated meeting | The CDC pages do not directly define this probability for our random meetings. |
| `exposed_days` | Time from infection to becoming infectious | Incubation duration is not necessarily latent duration. |
| `infectious_days` | Time during which the resident can transmit | Infectiousness varies by person and is not always constant. |
| `meetings_per_day` | Frequency of eligible simulated encounters | A clinical overview does not establish the contact frequency for our imaginary town. |
| Vaccination setting | How the model represents vaccine protection | A percentage vaccinated is not automatically the same as a percentage fully protected. |
