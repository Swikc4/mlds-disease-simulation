# Week 1 Math and Data Basics

## 1. Counts vs. proportions
A **count** is how many: 12 students.
A **proportion** is a part of a total: if 12 of 30 students brought lunch, the proportion is `12 / 30 = 0.4`.

A **percentage** multiplies that proportion by 100: `0.4 × 100 = 40%`. Counts and percentages are different: always check which you are using.

## 2. Change and rate
**Change** = new value − old value.

For example, a store sold 15 items Monday and 19 Tuesday:
- Change = `19 - 15 = 4` items.
- Percentage change = `(19 - 15) / 15 × 100 ≈ 26.7%`.

A **rate** describes a change relative to something, often time (for example, 6 pages per hour). Always look at the units and the time period.

## 3. Positive, negative, and zero
- Positive change: something increased.
- Negative change: something decreased.
- Zero change: it stayed the same.

Be careful: a negative **change** doesn't mean the original count is negative.

## 4. Multiplication and fractions
Suppose a recipe needs `3 / 4` cup of milk per batch.
- Two batches need `2 × 3/4 = 1.5` cups.
- Half a batch needs `0.5 × 3/4 = 0.375` cup.

Fractions are useful for describing parts of a group and scaling values.

## 5. Understanding a formula
`new_amount = old_amount + added - removed`

Read it in words: "The new amount equals what we had, plus what was added, minus what was removed."

When reading any formula, ask:
1. What does each variable represent?
2. What units does it use?
3. What would happen if an input becomes larger?
4. Is the resulting value reasonable?

These questions matter more than memorizing symbols.

## 6. Time steps and assumptions
A simulation calculates what could happen after a step (such as one day), then repeats. An **assumption** is a chosen simplification. For example, a budget projection might assume a person saves the same amount each week. Real life is usually less predictable.

**Important:** An illustration or an assumption is not a measured real-world fact. The values for our project are learning assumptions unless backed by a source.

## 7. How to read a graph
Before interpreting a graph:
- Read its title.
- Check x-axis and y-axis labels.
- Look at the units (people, percent, days, etc.).
- Notice whether the values rise, fall, or stay flat.
- Avoid saying a trend *caused* something unless the evidence supports that claim.

A graph of two trends rising together does not automatically show cause and effect.

## 8. A little about models
A **model** is a simplified representation of a system. It uses rules and assumptions to explore what may happen. A model can be useful without perfectly describing the real world.

Different models track different things; adding more detail can make a model harder to understand. In our project, you will meet SIR and SEIR, but these notes deliberately do **not** provide their equations, code, or final graphs. Work through those in the assignment and the research sources.
